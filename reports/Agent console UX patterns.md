# Make the console quiet, verifiable, and unanchored

An excellent agent operations console does three things that most current tools do only partly. It stays visually silent while agents are handling work and spends colour only on the few items that need a person. It turns every agent claim into something cheap to verify: a quote, a source, and whether the write was confirmed by read-back. And it asks the human for a decision without anchoring them on the agent's answer. The evidence for the first comes from industrial alarm management and clinical alert-fatigue research, where unactionable and repeated alerts drive people to ignore all of them. The evidence for the second and third comes from human-AI decision studies: explanations raise acceptance of AI advice *whether or not it is right*, and they reduce over-reliance only when checking them is cheaper than redoing the task. Asking people for a provisional view before showing the AI's answer cuts blind agreement and, in one radiology study, cost no extra time. The product patterns to copy are Braintrust for eval runs (score columns with header stats, saved views like "Errors", one click from an aggregate to the failing case), Langfuse and incident.io for timelines (a short narrative over a full log, shape first and detail on demand), Stripe Radar and Ramp for review queues (named recommendation labels with defined meanings, stated consequences, related records), and GOV.UK, Carbon and Primer for type, colour and status. For our console this means one concrete reversal: the decision page currently pre-selects the agent's recommendation (`src/ops/pages.ts:147`, commit `a7333e4`), and the research says that is the design that maximises anchoring. The full spec at the end ranks that change first.

## Colour belongs only to states that need a person

The oldest and best-tested idea here comes from process control, not software. The ISA-101 HMI standard uses a neutral base and saves colour, motion and blinking for abnormal situations; practitioners summarise it as "a gray screen signifies a healthy process", while warning that the real rule is a test applied to every colour: does it call attention to the abnormal? ([Control Engineering](https://www.controleng.com/high-performance-hmis-designs-to-improve-operator-effectiveness/)). Stephen Few's dashboard canon reaches the same place from a different direction, listing misused colour, clutter, and numbers without context among the common errors, and putting the most important item at the top left ([UXmatters review](https://uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2007/04/book-review-information-dashboard-design.php)). GOV.UK applies the idea to status tags. Its task list shows "Completed" as plain text with no tag "so tasks that still need action stand out", reserves red backgrounds for errors, and advises starting with the fewest statuses because "the more you add, the harder it is for users to remember them" ([GOV.UK task list](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/complete-multiple-tasks/), [GOV.UK Tag](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/tag/)).

The reason this matters for an agent console is alert fatigue, and the numbers are stark. A 2025 meta-analysis of 44 studies found clinicians override drug-interaction alerts **79.8%** of the time ([JMIR preprint](https://preprints.jmir.org/preprint/88578)). More useful than the headline is *why*: Ancker et al. found that the chance of accepting a reminder fell **30% for each extra reminder per encounter** and 10% for every 5-point rise in the share of repeats, and that acceptance tracked repetition and complexity rather than workload ([AHRQ PSNet](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/effects-workload-work-complexity-and-repeated-alerts-alert-fatigue-clinical-decision-support)). Google's SRE book turns this into an operating rule: every page should be actionable, alerts should describe symptoms rather than causes, and signals fall into three tiers of page, ticket and log ([SRECon16 slides](https://www.usenix.net/sites/default/files/conference/protected-files/srecon16europe_slides_rabenstein.pdf)). Industrial alarm benchmarks put the sustainable rate at about **one alarm per operator per ten minutes** ([Emerson](https://d1-live.emerson.com/documents/automation/article-alarm-management-by-numbers-deltav-en-38292.pdf)). That figure comes from control rooms. Applying it to office work is an extrapolation, but it gives a useful ceiling: if the inbox routinely gains more than about ten "needs you" items an hour, the escalation threshold is too loose.

Translated to our console, "needs you" must mean that the agent cannot or must not act and a person has to. A failed read-back on a case the agent then escalated is not a second alert; it is evidence inside the one escalation. Each case appears once in the inbox, with its reason updated rather than duplicated, because repeats are what wear down responses. The reason is written as a symptom the tenant or landlord would recognise ("Tenant has had no heating for 26 hours") rather than a cause ("calendar write failed"). Everything the agent handled normally stays grey.

Queue products show how to order and label that one list. Zendesk teams sort by SLA so the items closest to breach come first, but breached tickets lose their countdown and slip out of view, so guides recommend a separate "breached" group ([eesel guide](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/zendesk-sla-view-column-sort-by-breach)). GitHub gives every notification an explicit reason label ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/subscriptions-and-notifications/concepts/about-notifications)). Front treats Open, Waiting, Snoozed and Resolved as shared states ([Front help](https://help.front.com/en/articles/2134)). Linear Triage has single-key verbs, and its snooze wakes early when new activity arrives ([Linear Triage](https://linear.app/docs/triage), [Linear Snooze](https://linear.app/docs/snooze)). Together these give the inbox row a fixed shape: the reason, who has the ball (agent, us, tenant, landlord or contractor), and the deadline. "Waiting on tenant" is a watch state, not a "needs you" state. Endsley's situation-awareness model names what most consoles leave out. Operators need perception (what state is the case in), comprehension (why does it matter) and projection (what happens next, and when), and each level depends on the one before it ([NZ CAA guidance](https://www.aviation.govt.nz/assets/safety/human-factors/situational-awareness-guidance.pdf)). The "next step and when" field is the projection level, and it is the one most consoles leave out.

## Agent timelines need a narrative layer over a full log

Every major observability tool has settled on a run list followed by a split view: a navigator on the left and the selected step's details on the right. The recent change is that they now offer several equal views of the same run. Langfuse added a timeline in 2024, a toggle between tree and timeline in 2025, and in August 2026 a "responsive timeline" that fits the whole run on screen, colours rows by event type and zooms on demand. Langfuse's stated reason is that users had been scrolling "hoping to recognise the part that mattered", and the new goal is to see "the shape of the run first, then zoom in" ([Langfuse changelog](https://langfuse.com/changelog/2026-08-28-responsive-timeline)). Its graph view collapses repeated steps into one node with a counter ([Langfuse graph modes](https://langfuse.com/changelog/2026-07-13-graph-view-modes)). AgentOps puts errors in the waterfall as their own event type rather than inside tool output ([AgentOps docs](https://docs.agentops.ai/v2/usage/dashboard-info)). The OpenAI Agents SDK gives guardrails their own `guardrail_span` in the trace, so a policy check is a node in the trace and not a line hidden in a log ([OpenAI Agents SDK tracing](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/tracing/)).

These tools are built for engineers, and their known failures are what an ops person would also hit. PostHog calls raw traces "a bag of JSON" and re-renders them as a compact tree before summarising, because the metadata distracts ([PostHog blog](https://posthog.com/blog/llm-analytics-clustering-how-it-works.md)). Langfuse's guidance for product teams goes the other way: session replay that reads "top to bottom like a transcript", with any turn openable to show what happened underneath ([Langfuse for product teams](https://langfuse.com/resources/engineering/langfuse-for-product-teams)). incident.io gives the clearest model for a non-engineer. In 2024 it narrowed its default timeline to status changes and pinned messages so it "reads like a story rather than a log of events" ([incident.io changelog](https://incident.io/changelog/tell-the-full-story-with-your-incident-timeline)). Its API separates "the curated narrative ... the handful of moments that explain what happened" from the activity log "which records everything", and each narrative item keeps a link back to its log entry ([incident.io API docs](https://docs.incident.io/api-reference/incident-timeline-items-v2.md)). Salesforce puts "Upcoming & Overdue" above the history ([Trailhead](https://trailhead.salesforce.com/content/learn/modules/lightning-experience-productivity/manage-your-tasks-events-and-email)). GitHub's audit log now records `actor_is_agent` separately from `actor_is_bot` ([GitHub Enterprise Server 3.20](https://docs.github.com/en/enterprise-server@3.20/organizations/keeping-your-organization-secure/managing-security-settings-for-your-organization/audit-log-events-for-your-organization)).

Together these give the case page a definite structure. At the top is a header with the state, who has the ball, and the next step with its time. Below it, "Coming up" lists scheduled follow-ups. Then comes a narrative timeline limited to messages, actions with real-world effect, policy denials, escalations and human decisions. A collapsed full log holds tool calls, lookups and retries, with runs of similar events folded into chips such as "6 lookups · 2 min". Every entry names its actor (AI agent, staff member by name, system, or the outside party) and is stamped with when the event happened, not when it was recorded.

One gap in the market is an opportunity for us. None of the tools surveyed documents a "confirmed by read-back" state; they show success or error. Our agents already confirm writes by reading them back, so every action can carry one of three provenance states: **confirmed** (read back and matched), **not confirmed** (the write returned but the read-back disagreed or never happened), and **failed**. A policy denial is a fourth kind of event, not a failure. It is the system working as intended, so it gets its own shape and colour and is never shown as a red error. Lakera Guard models this cleanly: detections are their own filterable view, each shows the check and the content it screened, and the product only flags while the application decides ([Lakera docs](https://docs.lakera.ai/docs/platform)).

## Recommendations anchor people, so the agent's answer comes last

The human-factors research is consistent and uncomfortable. Automation bias affects novices and experts alike and "cannot be prevented by training or instructions", and complacency increases when people are juggling other work, which describes an ops desk exactly ([Parasuraman & Manzey 2010](https://depositonce.tu-berlin.de/items/0c787d91-6021-480b-a333-009e0d1d0092/full)). Bansal et al. found that explanations did not improve human-AI team performance; they "increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness" ([arXiv 2006.14779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779)). Buçinca et al. found that people rarely engage analytically with each recommendation and fall back on heuristics about when to trust the AI. Cognitive forcing designs reduced over-reliance: deciding before seeing the AI, slowing down, or choosing when to see it. Users liked those designs less ([Harvard](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2021/bucinca2021trust.shtml)). Two results show the way out. Vasconcelos et al. (5 studies, N=731) found that explanations do reduce over-reliance when they are **cheaper to verify than doing the task**, and when stakes are higher ([arXiv 2212.06823](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.06823)). Fogliato et al. had 19 radiologists record a provisional answer before seeing the AI: they agreed with it less often whether it was right or wrong, and the two-step workflow **did not lengthen task time** ([arXiv 2205.09696](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.09696)).

The design consequence is direct. A pre-selected radio button is the strongest anchor a page can apply, and our decision page uses it today. The fix keeps the recommendation visible but moves it below the evidence and removes the default selection, so the person makes one active choice. Fogliato's result says this costs no time. For the irreversible or harmful option, such as releasing money, sharing tenant data in response to a "legal" request, or changing bank details, add friction in proportion to the stakes: a one-line typed reason before the button works. Safe options like "hold" and "ask the agent for more" stay one click. Vasconcelos's result sets the standard for evidence: every claim links to the exact quote and source it rests on, so checking it takes a click and not a re-investigation. Zhang et al. add that confidence information calibrates trust but is "not sufficient" on its own; the human's value lies in knowledge the AI lacks ([arXiv 2001.02114](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.02114)). So the page lists what the agent *could not* check, such as a phone call, a landlord relationship or local knowledge, because that is where the operator beats the model.

Industry guidance and production review queues agree with this. Google PAIR warns that numeric confidence assumes users understand probability, that each confidence category needs a stated action, and that in high-stakes decisions you should never show "what" without "why" ([PAIR Guidebook](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/explainability-trust/)). Microsoft's HAX guidelines that apply here are "support efficient correction", "scope services when in doubt" and "convey the consequences of user actions" ([MSR](https://www.microsoft.com/research/project/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/)). Ramp uses exactly three labels, "Approval recommended", "Review recommended" and "Rejection recommended". "Review" explicitly covers the case where the agent lacked context, each rationale cites the relevant policy section, and reviewers keep final authority with a thumbs-up or thumbs-down on the recommendation ([Ramp support](https://support.ramp.com/spend-request-review-predictions)). Stripe Radar puts risk insights and **related payments** that share an email, IP or card into every review. Its docs warn that "a completed refund is permanent". When it does show confidence, it ties each label to an observed outcome rate (Very high = 72% chance of a dispute) rather than to a raw model score ([Stripe docs](https://docs.stripe.com/radar/reviews.md)). Anthropic's agent framework says humans should keep control "before high-stakes decisions", and separates reads, which need no approval, from modifications, which do ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/our-framework-for-developing-safe-and-trustworthy-agents)). Operator asks for confirmation before consequential steps such as sending an email ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-operator/)).

Clinical handoff research supplies the packet structure. A 2025 BMJ Quality & Safety review of 12 tools found that SBAR and I-PASS "probably improve patient safety", with I-PASS having the stronger evidence ([BMJ Qual Saf](https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/34/10/680)). Mapped onto an escalation, SBAR gives this order: the decision as a question with its deadline and what happens if nobody acts; the background, including the *raw* triggering message; what the agent did and deliberately did not do; the assessment, split into established, unverified and unknown; and finally the recommendation. Showing the raw source matters for a lettings agency in particular, because emails claiming to come from solicitors, police or landlords are the obvious route for prompt injection, and a paraphrase hides it. Two limits apply. None of this evidence comes from back-office operations; it comes from lab tasks and radiology. And no study found here measures quote-level citation interfaces directly; that recommendation rests on the Vasconcelos verification-cost result and on product practice.

## Eval runs should read like Braintrust and group by why cases stopped

Braintrust is the reference design for results across many cases. Its case table puts one column per scorer with summary stats in the header. It ships built-in views ("Errors", "Scorer errors", "Unreviewed", "Assigned to me"), and clicking a summary card opens a filter for "X regressions" against a baseline matched by input, with green and red deltas and an "Order by regressions" sort ([Braintrust interpret](https://www.braintrust.dev/docs/guides/evals/interpret), [Braintrust compare](https://www.braintrust.dev/docs/evaluate/compare-experiments)). Classifier columns can be grouped, which is how you see *which kind* of case does worse. Patronus offers a fixed base taxonomy of 20+ failure modes that users extend ([Patronus taxonomy](https://docs.patronus.ai/docs/percival/error-taxonomy)). PostHog clusters traces with an LLM and labels each cluster ([PostHog blog](https://posthog.com/blog/llm-analytics-clustering-how-it-works.md)). Galileo tracks tool error rate and action completion as agent metrics ([Galileo](https://galileo.ai/blog/galileo-agent-reliability-platform)).

For a 50-case run, the lesson is to separate two questions the current page blends: *how did each case end* and *was that ending right*. How a case ended needs a small, fixed stop-reason taxonomy that can be counted and grouped. Free text cannot be grouped, and LLM-written clusters are a nice-to-have. Whether the ending was right is the judge score and its critical failures. Crossing the two answers the useful questions directly, for example "every handed-to-person case scored 85+, but three resolved-by-agent cases scored under 60". The stop reason must also treat "escalated" and "did nothing" as legitimate outcomes, so the page does not read every non-action as a failure. The top of the page holds three or four summary figures, each a link to the filtered table, plus a delta against the previous run when one exists. Clicking a row opens that case's timeline. One gap: none of the tools reviewed documents a score histogram, so Braintrust's header stats are the safer pattern.

## Calm craft comes from 14px type, an 8px rhythm and five statuses

Product design systems agree on the numbers. Primer, Atlassian and Carbon all use **14px with a 20px line height** as the default body and table-cell size, 12px for captions, and a few semibold heading steps (16, 20, 24) ([Primer](https://primer.style/product/primitives/typography/), [Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/foundations/typography), [Carbon data table](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/data-table/style/)). GOV.UK uses 19px because it serves the general public; an internal ops tool is closer to Primer than to GOV.UK ([GOV.UK type scale](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/styles/type-scale/)). Linear uses one family in two optical cuts, Inter Display for headings and Inter for everything else ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)), and Atlassian keeps its expressive brand font for marketing only. Our console currently uses 15px body text with Newsreader serif headings at 30–32px (`src/ops/pages.ts:38-63`, `:138`). That is a valid editorial voice, but it does not match how working consoles set type, and the serif reaches body content such as the agent's letters and recommendations. GOV.UK moved its tags from uppercase to sentence case in regular weight after research found uppercase harder to read ([GOV.UK Tag](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/tag/)). `tabular-nums` keeps columns and counters from jittering ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/font-variant-numeric)).

Spacing follows a 4px base with an 8px rhythm, and Carbon's guidance that "spacing can group related content and create sections without lines" argues against card soup ([Carbon spacing](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/spacing/overview/)). GOV.UK says cards are for repeated objects of the same type and should not hold small amounts of information ([GOV.UK Summary list](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/summary-list/)). Carbon's default table rows are 40px with 16px cell padding ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/data-table/style/)). NN/g recommends hairlines or zebra striping plus a hover state, a frozen header, and a human-readable first column ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)). Status needs more than colour: Carbon pairs shape or icon with a label and requires 3:1 contrast against the background ([Carbon status](https://carbondesignsystem.com/patterns/status-indicator-pattern/), [WCAG 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). Each semantic role needs a foreground, a muted background and a border token, and Radix maps these to scale steps 11, 3 and 6–7 ([Radix](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors/docs/palette-composition/understanding-the-scale)). Click targets should be at least 24px ([WCAG 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)). For live eval runs, WCAG 2.2.2 requires a pause control on auto-updating content with no five-second exception ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)). Carbon reserves skeletons for first load and inline indicators for single actions ([Carbon loading](https://carbondesignsystem.com/patterns/loading-pattern/)). GOV.UK's writing rules complete the system: sentence case, numerals, "5:30pm", "cannot" rather than "can't", and one notification banner per page ([GOV.UK style](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/), [GOV.UK banner](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/notification-banner/)).

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. Visual polish matters less than two structural choices most agent consoles get wrong: what earns colour, and where the agent's opinion sits on the decision page. Our build is already close on the first. The calmer portal in `a7333e4` leads with who has the ball and flags only exceptions. It is wrong on the second: pre-selecting the recommendation is the design the research identifies as maximising blind acceptance, and the fix is cheap and, per Fogliato, costs the operator no time. The less obvious opportunity is provenance. The observability tools record "success" and "error", but none surveyed distinguishes a write the system *confirmed by reading it back* from one it merely claimed, or treats a deterministic policy denial as correct behaviour rather than failure. Those two distinctions match how our agents actually work, so they are both the most honest thing the console can show and the clearest difference from generic trace viewers.

One caveat holds across the report. Most quantitative evidence comes from control rooms, hospitals and lab tasks rather than lettings operations, and several product details come from vendor docs or secondary summaries. The rules below are well-grounded starting points, not tuned values. The test for whether they work here is the one ISA-18.2 recommends: watch which escalations operators repeatedly dismiss, and treat each one as a threshold to fix.

### Spacing and layout

Tokens: `--s1:4px --s2:8px --s3:12px --s4:16px --s5:24px --s6:32px --s7:48px`. Use 4–8px inside a key/value pair, 8–12px between rows and 32–48px between sections. Page gutter is 16px on phone and 24–32px on desktop. Content max-width is about 960px for the case and decision pages and full width for the eval table. Separate sections with a heading plus space, or a single 1px hairline. Cards are only for repeated objects of the same type (an inbox row, a related case), and never nest one card inside another. Table rows are 40px with 16px horizontal padding, a sticky 600-weight header in `--fg-muted`, hairline rules (not zebra), a hover background, and numbers right-aligned. Below 640px, rows collapse to stacked key/value blocks with the identifier and status on the first line. Targets are at least 24px.

**Status vocabulary (five, each = shape + sentence-case adjective label):**

| Status | Shape | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Needs you | ● filled dot | `--needs-*` tag, the only filled tag in the inbox |
| Problem (failed / not confirmed) | ✕ / ◐ | `--danger-*` tag |
| Working | small dot, 1.5s opacity pulse, removed under `prefers-reduced-motion` | `--info-fg` text |
| Waiting on {tenant, landlord, contractor} | ◷ clock | plain `--fg-muted` text |
| Done | none | plain text, no tag |

Provenance glyphs used on timeline actions: `✓ Confirmed` (read back and matched, `--ok-fg`), `◐ Not confirmed` (write returned, read-back missing or different, `--danger-fg`), `✕ Failed` (`--danger-fg`), `⊘ Denied by policy · {rule name}` (`--policy-fg`). A denial is never red.

### Stop-reason taxonomy (fixed, one per case, computed from events)

| Code | Label | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | Resolved by agent | all real-world writes confirmed, no open task |
| `handed_over` | Handed to a person | open or closed human task created |
| `waiting` | Waiting on someone else | message sent, reply or follow-up scheduled |
| `no_action` | Chose to do nothing | agent decided no action is needed, with a reason |
| `policy_stop` | Stopped by policy | last attempted write denied and no alternative taken |
| `unconfirmed` | Action not confirmed | any write failed or not confirmed by read-back |
| `no_decision` | Agent did not finish | error, timeout, step limit |

Precedence when several apply: `no_decision` > `unconfirmed` > `handed_over` > `policy_stop` > `waiting` > `no_action` > `resolved`. Show the label in the case header, as an inbox row chip (coloured only for `unconfirmed` and `no_decision`), and as a groupable column on the eval page.

### Page 1: Decisions inbox (`/ops`)

1. h1 is a sentence stating the count: "2 decisions need you" or "Nothing needs you right now" (empty state, plus "Last agent activity 4:12pm").
2. **Needs you**: one row per case. Group "Past deadline" pinned first, then sort by deadline ascending, then severity, then age. Each row is laid out as: reason written as a symptom (14/20, primary) · property and person (13, muted) · deadline as relative time with tabular nums · ball-holder · recommendation label as plain text ("Agent suggests: hold") · "Decide" link. No recommendation colour in the list.
3. **Watch**: open cases where the agent or another party has the ball. Plain rows, coloured only when the stop reason is `unconfirmed` or `no_decision`.
4. Done today: collapsed, with a count.
5. Header counts show context, e.g. "3 need you (1 past deadline)".

### Page 2: Case page (`/ops/cases/:id`)

1. Breadcrumb, then hero line (24/32): "{Who has the ball} until {when}: {why}". The stop-reason tag sits beside it.
2. Facts strip (summary list with hairlines): property, tenant, landlord, contractor, job ref, opened.
3. **Coming up**: scheduled follow-ups and deadlines, with overdue items first.
4. **What happened**: a narrative timeline, newest at the bottom, grouped into turns (call, agent run, decision). Each entry is `time · actor label (AI agent / {staff name} / system / {tenant name}) · plain-language sentence · provenance glyph`. Inbound and outbound messages are shown as quotes, with the full text behind `<details>`. Repeated lookups and retries fold into one chip: "6 lookups · 2 min".
5. **Full agent log**: collapsed `<details>` holding every tool call with arguments, result and read-back, in a mono 13px table. Engineers only.
6. Open decision, if any: a link card to page 3. Do not duplicate the form here.

### Page 3: Decision page (`/ops/tasks/:id`) in SBAR order

1. **Question** (hero 24/32) phrased with its options, then a meta line: deadline · "If nobody decides: {default consequence}" · team and priority.
2. **Why this came to you**: one sentence giving the escalation reason, followed by the raw triggering message quoted verbatim with sender, channel and time. The source is always shown, never only the paraphrase.
3. **Already done / deliberately not done**: two short lists with provenance glyphs ("✓ Booked contractor slot, confirmed"; "Not done: did not reply to sender; did not release funds"; "⊘ Denied by policy: payment over £500 limit").
4. **Evidence** in three labelled groups. *Established*: each item is claim + quote + source link + time. *Unverified or conflicting*. *Could not check (your knowledge needed)*: phone call, landlord relationship, local knowledge. Add related cases sharing phone, email, bank account or property where available.
5. **Agent's recommendation**, placed here, after the evidence. Use one of three labels: *Agent recommends {option}* / *Needs your judgement* (use this when the agent lacked context) / *Agent recommends against {option}*. Follow with a one-line reason, "What would change this:", and the policy or rule cited. No percentages.
6. **Your decision**: radio options with **nothing pre-selected**. Each option has a verb + object label and a consequence line naming target, amount and reversibility ("Send £1,250 to account ending 4411. Cannot be undone."). Options marked irreversible or high-risk reveal a required "Reason" field (at least 10 characters) before submit is enabled, server-side validated. Always include "Ask the agent for more" and "Hold until {date}" as one-click safe options. Submit is a single primary button whose label echoes the selection.
7. Optional, after submitting: "Was the agent's recommendation right? Yes / No (+ reason)", recorded on the case timeline as a human event.

### Page 4: Eval run (`/ops/evals/:id`)

1. h1: run name, then a live line "38 of 50 done · 6 running · 1m 42s" in tabular nums, with a **Pause live updates** control. Update the changed region only, through one `aria-live="polite"` region. Do not use skeletons after first load.
2. **Four summary figures**, each a link that filters the table: *Mean judge score* (with delta vs previous run if one exists) · *Critical failures* (count, `--danger-fg` only if > 0) · *Handed to a person* (count) · *Actions not confirmed* (count). Use metric type at 24/32 600, not a 44px serif.
3. **How cases ended**: a single horizontal bar or a plain count list by stop reason, with each segment linked to the filtered table. No pies or gauges.
4. **Why cases stopped short**: keep the existing grouping by policy rule and by tool (`batchView.ts:42–60`), but key each group to a stop-reason code and show "{rule} · {n} cases", expandable to the case links.
5. **Case table**: columns are case (frozen, human-readable) · stop reason (groupable) · judge score (right-aligned, red only when below 60 or critical) · critical/red-flag first line · Δ vs previous (when a baseline exists) · cost. Header cells show stats ("Score · mean 78"). Preset views sit above the table as links: All · Low score · Critical · Handed to a person · Not confirmed · Denied by policy · Agent did not finish. Clicking a row opens the case page timeline.
6. Tool calls asked for: move below the table into a collapsed `<details>`.

### Writing rules

Use sentence case everywhere. Use numerals, "£1,250", "5:30pm", "2 June", and "cannot". Buttons are a verb and an object, 3 words or fewer. Headings state the point ("2 decisions need you"), not the widget name. Allow at most one banner per page. Describe a reason by its symptom first ("Tenant has had no heating for 26 hours"), and give the internal cause only in the log. Never print raw JSON outside the full agent log.
